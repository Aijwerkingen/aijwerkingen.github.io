When ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, scientists put it to work almost immediately - writing essays, summarising literature, drafting papers, even writing code for statistical analyses. Some celebrated. Many worried. A small group of researchers - including the team behind this site - decided the conversation needed structure.

So in early 2023, they published [five priorities for research with ChatGPT](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-023-00288-7) in Nature. Their starting point was simple and, at the time, unusual: banning the technology wouldn't work, so the real question was how to use it responsibly. Today, with living guidelines adopted as official European guidance, those five priorities read like the seed of everything that followed.

## 1. Hold on to human verification

ChatGPT's text is convincing - and often wrong. The authors put it to the test: asked about depression relapse after treatment, the chatbot claimed treatment effects are typically long-lasting, when high-quality studies put the relapse risk at 29–51% within the first 2–4 years. Asked to summarise their own systematic review, it said the review covered 46 studies - it covered 69 - and exaggerated the treatment's effectiveness.

The conclusion: expert fact-checking stays indispensable, and humans remain accountable for science, whatever the tool.

## 2. Develop rules for accountability

Detecting AI-written text is an arms race that can't be won. The article argued for transparency instead: papers should state clearly whether and how AI was used - which models, and to what extent. And AI systems should not be authors, because they cannot be held accountable.

## 3. Invest in truly open LLMs

The most capable models are proprietary, and their training data is opaque - the authors note ChatGPT could say nothing about several renowned scientists with h-indices above 80. Their fix: real investment in open, non-profit AI, pointing to the BigScience collaboration's BLOOM model as proof it can be done.

## 4. Embrace the benefits

This wasn't doom-mongering. AI could accelerate innovation, free researchers for new experimental designs, and - through "intelligent partnerships" between people and machines - outperform what either does alone. The task is deciding which human skills stay essential.

## 5. Widen the debate

Their final ask: every research group should try ChatGPT for themselves and talk about it, and an international forum should keep the conversation going - scientists, tech companies, funders, publishers and the public.

## Where that led

Those five priorities grew into the living guidelines - updated as the technology changes - and the approach is now [official European guidance](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/download/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en?filename=ec_rtd_ai-guidelines.pdf), in its third edition (we wrote about that [here](/blog/living-guidelines-for-ai-in-research)). The rules for AI in science didn't come out of nowhere: they started with a handful of scientists insisting, in early 2023, that humans stay in charge.

## Same experts, same spirit

This site comes from the same team - and the same conviction that oversight of AI is a public-interest question on both fronts: how scientists use AI, and how AI affects people. If a conversation with an AI tool left you feeling worse, [report it](/report) - that's the people-facing side of the same concern.
